## P1｜背景与问题（第1–3页）

Good morning, everyone. Today we are reviewing a 2020 paper about disposable masks during the early COVID-19 shortage. Our question is not simply whether a mask can be used again. It is what evidence would be needed before anyone could call a reuse method safe.

The paper is *Can Masks Be Reused After Hot Water Decontamination During the COVID-19 Pandemic?*, by Dan Wang and colleagues, published in *Engineering*. It reports that Chinese mask production was eight million a day on the twenty-third of January 2020, for a population of more than 1.4 billion. This is the historical context described by the authors, not a description of today's supply.

The manuscript was received on the twenty-sixth of May and accepted on the twenty-ninth. That is a three-day interval between the recorded dates. Those dates alone do not tell us how the peer review was conducted.

The authors investigated hot-water treatment followed by drying and charge regeneration. We will describe their experiments, not recommend a household procedure. Three separate questions guide our review. First, was virus inactivation directly demonstrated? Second, was filtration retained under the specified tests? Third, what happened after repeated treatment and actual wear?

These questions have different answers. In particular, good filtration does not by itself establish disinfection or a good face seal. Our next speaker will explain what the procedure study actually measured.

## P2｜处理条件与电荷测量（第4–6页）

The authors studied three containers: an aluminium basin, a plastic lunch box, and a stainless-steel thermos. They measured how the water temperature changed during a thirty-minute treatment. Their temperature target came from historical guidance cited in the paper, not from a direct virus challenge performed in this study.

In the tested conditions, the water started near ninety degrees Celsius. After thirty minutes, the basin and lunch box were around sixty degrees, and the thermos was around eighty-five. These observations depend on the containers, water volume and experimental conditions. They are not a guarantee for any container at home.

The paper also shows fluorescence images of a used mask before and after treatment. Reduced visible fluorescence is evidence about the observed contamination signal; it is not a measurement of infectious virus remaining on the mask.

Next, the authors used an electrostatic field meter to examine changes in the mask material. They reported reduced electrostatic readings after soaking and recovery after drying. Relative to a new mask, the reported recovery was about sixty percent after ten hours of natural drying and about ninety percent after ten minutes with a hair dryer. These percentages concern the electrostatic measurement, not the percentage of viruses killed or the percentage of filtration restored.

Electrostatic capture can contribute to filtration without requiring an equally dense mechanical filter. But it is not the only way to collect small particles. We should also avoid saying that rapid drying was proved to prevent bacterial growth: that was not a directly measured endpoint here.

The important distinction is between a measured temperature or electric-field reading and a demonstrated health outcome. Our next speaker will discuss the filtration tests.

## P3｜过滤机制与数据（第7–10页）

A typical surgical mask has an outer layer, a middle filtering layer, and an inner layer. The paper describes a polypropylene melt-blown filter with fine fibres. Its diagrams illustrate several capture mechanisms, including diffusion, interception, inertial impaction, gravitational settling, and electrostatic attraction.

For very small particles, Brownian diffusion can bring particles into contact with fibres. Electrostatic forces can also help capture them. Therefore, the old claim that static electricity is the only mechanism for capturing nano-sized particles is too strong.

We must distinguish two test endpoints. Particle filtration efficiency, or PFE, was measured using a TSI 8130 instrument and sodium chloride aerosol. The stated count-median particle diameter was 0.075 plus or minus 0.020 micrometres. The nominal flow rates were thirty litres per minute for the disposable medical and surgical masks and eighty-five for the samples the paper calls KN95-grade. The stated tolerances were two and four litres per minute, respectively.

Bacterial filtration efficiency, or BFE, is a different endpoint based on bacterial control and test-effluent counts. We should not describe the NaCl instrument as if it measured both endpoints. The paper reports BFE above ninety-five percent for the regenerated JH and YB samples. It reports an average surgical-mask PFE of 92.3 percent and PFE above ninety-five percent for the tested 3M and KF samples. These are sample-specific findings under different test conditions, not universal mask ratings.

Five brands were tested over ten treatment cycles, with little change in filtration reported under those conditions. A separate pressurised-steam experiment reported average PFE values of 99.2 percent for 3M 1860 and 96.6 percent for KF94, after the stated treatment and charge regeneration.

There is a model-identification issue to flag: the initial sample section names 3M 9502, while the steam results name 3M 1860. We retain the authors' wording for each experiment rather than silently treating them as one model. KF94 is also a distinct designation; calling these samples KN95-grade in the paper does not establish interchangeability of certification standards.

So the evidence supports retained filtration in specified samples and tests. It does not yet answer all of the safety questions.

## P4｜真实佩戴、局限与收束（第11–13页）

The authors also examined waterproofing and actual wear. Their water test used a specified volume, pouring interval and vacuum-filtration procedure. They reported no seepage after ten cycles and showed microscopy images of the fibres. Those observations should be described within that laboratory test, not as a guarantee of complete protection in use.

After eight hours of wear and subsequent treatment, the paper reports a filtration decrease of 0.5 to twelve percent across fifteen surgical-mask samples, while all ten samples described as KN95 remained above ninety-five percent. The text reports that decrease as a percentage; we should not silently reinterpret it as percentage points without checking the underlying data.

Fluorescent penetrant observations led the authors to link performance changes to contamination and fibre wettability. This is a proposed explanation supported by those observations. It does not exclude every other source of degradation or prove that hot-water treatment can never contribute to damage.

The factory case is also worth distinguishing from a clinical trial. Zhejiang Runtu reported reducing mask consumption from one per person per day to one every three days, saving 122,500 masks over the reported period. That is a resource-use outcome. The paper does not establish a reduction in infection risk from that figure.

Our verdict is therefore limited evidence, not approval. The study reports filtration retention and practical deployment under emergency conditions. It does not directly measure virus inactivation, test face fit and seal, or show that every disposable mask can safely undergo the same treatment.

There is also a treatment-description inconsistency. Section 3.5 describes hot-water treatment after wear, whereas the conclusion refers to pressurised steam at 121 degrees for the worn-mask result. We cannot resolve that discrepancy from the text alone. It limits our ability to attribute that result to one specific procedure.

The engineering lesson is to separate acceptance criteria, measured results and missing evidence. Filtration, disinfection, fit and repeated-use durability are related, but they are not interchangeable. Emergency engineering still needs evidence: filtration alone does not establish safe reuse. Thank you.
